## Retail and shops

### Serving customers kindly

**What this looks at work** (53 words)

Shoppers notice how you greet them. A smile and a quick greeting make people feel welcome. Help them find what they need, or fetch someone who knows. If you do not know an answer, say so honestly. Example: a customer asks where the bread is, and you walk them to the right shelf.

**Try this** (36 words)

Say out loud how you would greet a customer walking into a shop. Then practise one short sentence to help them: "Good morning, how can I help you?" Repeat it three times until it feels natural.

### Handling cash and change

**What this looks at work** (50 words)

The till is the machine that takes payments. When someone pays cash, you count the money you got and work out their change out loud. Slow and careful beats fast and wrong. Example: a loaf costs eighteen rand and the customer hands you twenty, so you give back two rand.

**Try this** (35 words)

Take any eight small items around you. Give each one a price in rands, then add them up in your head. Check your total by counting on your fingers, slowly, until the answer is right.

### Stock counting and shelves

**What this looks at work** (51 words)

Stock means the goods a shop sells. You count items, check dates, and fill empty spaces on shelves so customers always find what they need. Tidying shelves also helps you spot what is running low. Example: you notice the milk shelf is half empty and bring more from the back room.

**Try this** (31 words)

Count the items in one cupboard or drawer at home. Write the number on any scrap of paper. Then count again to check your first number, and tidy the shelf afterwards.

### Keeping the shop clean

**What this looks at work** (54 words)

A clean shop feels safe and welcomes customers back. Cleaning means sweeping floors, wiping counters, and tidying spills straight away. You also take out rubbish before it smells. Example: a customer spills a drink, so you fetch a cloth and a warning sign before someone slips. Quick cleaning keeps everyone safe in the shop.

**Try this** (30 words)

Look around the room you are in now. Name three things you would clean first if a customer walked in. Say each one out loud, and why it matters most.

### Working a till or card machine

**What this looks at work** (50 words)

A till records a sale and prints a receipt. A card machine takes payment from a bank card or phone. You scan items, check the price on the screen, and hand over the receipt. Example: the card machine says approved, so you give the customer their slip and thank them.

**Try this** (31 words)

Pretend your hand is a card machine. Practise the steps out loud: greet, scan the item, say the price, ask card or cash, then thank the customer. Run through it twice.

### Talking clearly with people

**What this looks at work** (51 words)

Clear talking means simple words, a steady voice, and listening until the other person finishes. In a shop you explain prices and deals without confusion. If someone looks lost, ask a short question to help. Example: a customer asks about a special, and you explain the price and the closing time.

**Try this** (32 words)

Explain one thing you know well to an imaginary customer, in under one minute. Use short sentences. Imagine they interrupt with a question, and practise answering it calmly. Finish by thanking them.

## Cleaning

### Safe use of cleaning chemicals

**What this looks at work** (59 words)

Chemicals are strong liquids like bleach that clean well but can burn skin or eyes. You read the label, wear gloves, and never mix products together. Use each one only where the label says. Example: you never mix bleach with toilet cleaner, because the fumes can hurt your lungs. Keep chemicals locked away when you are not using them.

**Try this** (36 words)

Find one cleaning bottle at home and read its label out loud. Say what it cleans, what gear to wear, and one thing you must never do with it. Add one safety tip of your own.

### Mopping, dusting and sanitising

**What this looks at work** (56 words)

Dusting removes loose dirt from surfaces. Mopping cleans floors with water and cleaning liquid. Sanitising means wiping a surface in a way that kills germs, not just moving dirt around. Example: after cleaning a desk, you spray it and wipe it so it is safe to touch. Use a separate cloth for each kind of job.

**Try this** (35 words)

Take a cloth and wipe one small surface at home three times: first for dust, then with water, then the way you would sanitise it. Notice how each pass differs. Say what changes each time.

### Handling rubbish and recycling

**What this looks at work** (54 words)

Rubbish must leave a building safely and on time. You carry bags without tearing them, tie them well, and keep wet waste separate from dry recycling like paper and boxes. Example: you take kitchen bags to the outside bin, then flatten cardboard boxes for the recycling pile. Good sorting keeps the whole site clean.

**Try this** (36 words)

Walk through your home and sort five items into two groups: things to throw away, and things to recycle. Say out loud why each item belongs in its group. Repeat for every room if you can.

### Working to a checklist

**What this looks at work** (56 words)

A checklist lists every cleaning task in order, so nothing gets missed. You tick each task only when it is truly finished. Checklists help you work alone and stay honest. Example: an office list has bathrooms, kitchen, floors, and bins, and you finish each one before you go home. Your supervisor can check your work easily.

**Try this** (35 words)

Write four cleaning tasks you would do in one room, in the order you would do them. Read the list, then imagine ticking each task as you finish it. Practise saying each task out loud.

### Wearing protective gear

**What this looks at work** (53 words)

Protective gear keeps chemicals, dust, and germs off your body. It includes gloves, masks, aprons, and goggles. You wear the right gear before you start, and clean or store it after. Example: you put on gloves before using a strong toilet cleaner, and wash them afterwards. Ask for gear that fits you well.

**Try this** (36 words)

Name five pieces of protective gear you would wear for cleaning, and one job each one protects you from. Say your answers out loud, slowly and clearly. Then say which piece you would put on first.

### Reporting damage or hazards

**What this looks at work** (56 words)

A hazard is anything that could hurt someone, like a loose floor tile or a broken light. You report it to your supervisor straight away, and warn people nearby. Example: you find water on a bathroom floor, so you place a warning sign and tell your supervisor before anyone slips. Early reports prevent accidents and complaints.

**Try this** (35 words)

Think of three hazards you might find in a shop or office. For each one, practise the exact short sentence you would use to report it to a boss. Keep each report short and clear.

## Building and manual work

### Lifting and carrying safely

**What this looks at work** (55 words)

Bad lifting can injure your back for life. You bend your knees, keep the load close, and never twist while carrying. Ask for help with anything heavy or awkward. Example: a cement bag is too heavy, so you lift half at a time or ask a workmate to help. Practise this every time you lift.

**Try this** (30 words)

Stand up and practise lifting a light object properly: feet apart, bend your knees, keep your back straight, and hold the load close. Repeat three times until it feels natural.

### Measuring with a tape

**What this looks at work** (56 words)

A tape measure shows lengths in centimetres and metres. You measure twice before cutting, because wood and pipe cost money. You mark your measurement clearly so you do not lose it. Example: a shelf must fit a gap of ninety centimetres, so you check the gap and the shelf. Ask your supervisor to check difficult measurements.

**Try this** (30 words)

Measure three objects near you with your hand or a cloth as a guide. Say each length out loud. Then measure the same objects again to check your first answers.

### Using basic hand tools

**What this looks at work** (54 words)

Hand tools are things like hammers, screwdrivers, pliers, and drills. You pick the right tool for the job, hold it firmly, and keep it in good condition. Example: a loose door handle needs a screwdriver, not a hammer, and you tighten the screws until it stops wobbling. Always put tools back where they belong.

**Try this** (33 words)

Name five common hand tools and one job each one does. Say them out loud. Then find one tool at home and check it is clean and working properly. Test yourself again tomorrow.

### Following site safety rules

**What this looks at work** (54 words)

Building sites have rules that keep people safe: hard hats, safety boots, and clear walkways. You listen to the site briefing and follow every sign. Example: a helmet protects your head from falling objects, so you wear it from the moment you arrive until you leave. Never break a safety rule to save time.

**Try this** (36 words)

List five safety rules you would expect on a building site. Imagine you are new on day one, and practise asking your supervisor where the helmets are kept. Then say what each rule protects you from.

### Reading simple plans

**What this looks at work** (51 words)

A plan is a drawn map of what must be built. It uses simple symbols for doors, windows, and walls, with measurements in millimetres or centimetres. You read it before starting so you build the right thing. Example: the plan shows a door swinging inward, so you hang it that way.

**Try this** (38 words)

Draw a simple floor plan of one room you know well. Use a square for the room, and mark the door and windows. Say out loud what each mark means. Then say which wall you would build first.

### Working as a team on site

**What this looks at work** (53 words)

Site jobs are too big for one person. You agree who does what, speak up when something changes, and watch out for your workmates. Example: one person measures while another cuts, and you both check the length before the piece is fixed in place. Good teamwork keeps everyone safe and the job tidy.

**Try this** (36 words)

Plan a small imaginary job with a partner, like moving furniture. Say out loud what each of you would carry, and how you would tell each other about dangers. Finish by agreeing who checks the work.

## Office and admin work

### Typing and using a keyboard

**What this looks at work** (50 words)

Office work happens on a keyboard all day. You learn where the letters sit so you type without looking down. You also use keys like Enter, Tab, and Delete to move and fix text. Example: you type a short message quickly, then check it for mistakes before you send it.

**Try this** (33 words)

Place your fingers on a keyboard or a flat surface as if typing. Type or spell out one short sentence ten times. Focus on accuracy first, then speed. Practise a little every day.

### Filing and organising

**What this looks at work** (51 words)

Filing means putting documents where you can find them again. You name folders clearly, keep related papers together, and sort them in a sensible order. Good filing saves hours later. Example: invoices go in the invoices folder, sorted by month, so anyone can find one fast. Colleagues find things quickly too.

**Try this** (32 words)

Take five papers or items near you and group them by type. Give each group a clear name, and say out loud where you would file each group. Practise the names again.

### Writing clear emails

**What this looks at work** (51 words)

A clear email has a short subject line, a greeting, and one main point per paragraph. You say what you need and by when, then close politely. Example: you write to ask for a meeting on Thursday at ten, and you check the address before sending. Short emails get replies sooner.

**Try this** (35 words)

Write a short email on any scrap of paper asking a boss for one day of leave. Include a subject line, one clear reason, and a polite closing sentence. Read it once before you finish.

### Using spreadsheets

**What this looks at work** (53 words)

A spreadsheet is a grid of boxes that stores numbers and words. Rows go across, columns go down. You use it to add up costs and keep lists. Example: you type five prices into a column and use a simple sum to get the total. Spreadsheets also sort lists and draw simple charts.

**Try this** (36 words)

On any scrap of paper, draw a small grid of four boxes across and three down. Write three items and prices inside, then add up the prices below. Say the total out loud when you finish.

### Keeping records up to date

**What this looks at work** (55 words)

Records are lists and files that show what happened at work. You update them the same day, while details are fresh. Bosses trust people whose records are accurate. Example: after a delivery arrives, you note the date, the items, and any damage, before you start the next job. Old notes are easy to get wrong.

**Try this** (36 words)

Write down three things you did today, with times, as if recording them for work. Keep each note short and factual, with no opinions. Read them back to check. Then say what you would add tomorrow.

### Meeting deadlines

**What this looks at work** (50 words)

A deadline is the time something must be finished. You break big work into small steps, start early, and tell your boss early if a problem appears. Example: a report is due Friday, so you draft it by Wednesday and check it on Thursday morning. Telling people early builds trust.

**Try this** (30 words)

Pick one task you must finish this week. Say out loud the day you would start, the day it would be finished, and one problem that could slow you down.

## Driving and delivery

### A valid licence for the vehicle

**What this looks at work** (56 words)

You may only drive vehicles your licence allows. A code B licence covers small cars. Bigger vehicles need extra codes and tests. You carry your licence and check it stays valid. Example: you cannot deliver in a small truck without the right licence code, even if you can drive. Employers check your licence before you start.

**Try this** (37 words)

Say out loud what type of licence you would need for a small car, a delivery bakkie, and a truck. Then name one place you would check the rules. Say where you would keep your licence safe.

### Safe, defensive driving

**What this looks at work** (56 words)

Defensive driving means expecting other people to make mistakes. You keep a safe gap, check your mirrors often, and slow down in bad weather. You never rush to make up time. Example: a car pulls out in front of you, so you slow down instead of sounding the horn. Staying calm keeps you and others safe.

**Try this** (32 words)

Name five hazards you would watch for on a busy Cape Town street. Say each one out loud, and one calm action you would take for each. Pretend you are driving now.

### Keeping to routes and times

**What this looks at work** (53 words)

Deliveries follow a route, a set path through streets, and a time plan. You learn the route, allow for traffic, and phone ahead if you will be late. Example: your first stop closes at four, so you plan to arrive by half past three. Good planning keeps customers happy. Late calls prevent complaints.

**Try this** (37 words)

Plan a route for three imaginary stops from your home. Say the order you would visit them in, and give each stop a time. Explain why you chose that order. Say how long each stop would take.

### Loading and securing goods

**What this looks at work** (52 words)

Goods must be packed so they do not move or fall. You place heavy items low, spread weight evenly, and tie everything down. You also check nothing blocks your view. Example: boxes of glasses travel on top, wrapped, while heavy crates sit flat on the floor. Nothing should shift while you drive.

**Try this** (34 words)

Imagine loading five boxes of different weights into a car boot. Say out loud where you would put each one, and how you would stop them sliding. Say how you would tie them down.

### Checking the vehicle first

**What this looks at work** (52 words)

Before you drive, you walk around the vehicle and check tyres, lights, oil, and fuel. Small checks stop big breakdowns. You report anything wrong before leaving. Example: a tyre looks flat, so you check the pressure and fill it before the first delivery. A quick check keeps you safe and saves money.

**Try this** (35 words)

Name eight things you would check on a vehicle before a delivery trip. Say them in the order you would check them, starting from the front of the car. Use the same order every day.

### Using delivery apps and phones

**What this looks at work** (55 words)

Many delivery jobs run on a phone app. The app shows your stops, records delivery, and stores a photo or signature as proof. You keep the phone charged and handle it carefully. Example: you take a photo of the parcel at the door to prove it arrived. A flat phone can stop your whole trip.

**Try this** (30 words)

Practise describing a delivery out loud: where it goes, who receives it, and what proof you would keep. Imagine the customer is not home, and say what you would do.

## Cooking and food work

### Food hygiene and handwashing

**What this looks at work** (51 words)

Food hygiene means keeping food and yourself clean so people do not get sick. You wash your hands with soap before cooking and after touching rubbish or raw meat. You tie back long hair and stay home when ill. Example: you wash your hands for twenty seconds before you start chopping.

**Try this** (33 words)

Practise the handwashing steps out loud: wet your hands, add soap, scrub palms and backs, between fingers and nails, then rinse and dry. Say each step slowly. Clean hands protect everyone who eats.

### Safe knife skills

**What this looks at work** (58 words)

Knives are sharp tools that need calm hands. You keep fingers curled away from the blade, cut on a steady board, and never catch a falling knife. You carry it with the blade pointing down. Example: you hold an onion with fingertips tucked under, then slice slowly and evenly. A sharp knife is safer than a blunt one.

**Try this** (33 words)

With a pen as a pretend knife, practise a cutting motion on a book. Curl your free hand’s fingers under, like holding an onion. Repeat slowly ten times. Keep your fingers curled throughout.

### Following recipes and portions

**What this looks at work** (52 words)

A recipe lists ingredients and steps so the same dish tastes the same every time. You measure portions carefully, because steady quality keeps customers happy. Example: the recipe says two spoons of spice per portion, so you use the same spoon for every plate. Guests expect the same dish at every visit.

**Try this** (31 words)

Describe making a simple meal out loud, step by step, as if teaching a new helper. Pretend the recipe must feed four people, and say how you would share it out.

### Working cleanly under pressure

**What this looks at work** (53 words)

Kitchens get hot and busy, but you keep your space tidy as you work. You wipe spills, wash tools between tasks, and keep raw meat away from vegetables. Cleaning as you go saves time later. Example: while waiting for water to boil, you wash the chopping board. A tidy station keeps you fast.

**Try this** (33 words)

Imagine cooking two dishes at once. Say out loud the three tasks you would do while waiting for a pot to boil. Practise describing each step in order. Say them again more quickly.

### Safe use of kitchen equipment

**What this looks at work** (56 words)

Kitchen equipment like ovens, fryers, and slicers can cause serious burns and cuts. You read the instructions, keep handles turned in, and never leave hot oil alone. You switch machines off before cleaning. Example: you turn pot handles away from the edge so nobody knocks them over. Ask before using any machine for the first time.

**Try this** (34 words)

Name five pieces of kitchen equipment and one safety rule for each. Say them out loud. Then find one rule at home you already follow without thinking. Say which rule you would never break.

### Checking food dates and storage

**What this looks at work** (54 words)

Every packaged food carries a date telling you when it is best used by. You check dates when items arrive and move older items to the front. You keep cold food cold and dry food dry. Example: new yoghurt goes behind the older ones on the shelf. This stops spoiled food from being served.

**Try this** (34 words)

Find three packets in your kitchen and read their dates out loud. Say which one you would use first, and why you would choose it. Say what you would do with the oldest item.

## Hair and beauty

### Cleaning and sanitising tools

**What this looks at work** (52 words)

Hair and beauty tools touch skin, so they must be cleaned after every client. You wash, rinse, and soak tools in sanitising liquid that kills germs. Clean tools live in a closed container. Example: combs and scissors go into the sanitising solution, then dry on a clean towel. Clients notice clean tools.

**Try this** (33 words)

Name five tools a hairdresser uses and describe how you would clean each one. Say your answers out loud, in the order you would clean them. Say which one you would clean first.

### Basic cutting and styling

**What this looks at work** (56 words)

Basic cutting means trimming hair evenly and shaping it with steady hands. Styling means drying and arranging hair the way a client asked. You work slowly at first and check as you go. Example: you comb a section flat, hold it between two fingers, and trim the ends. Ask the client if the length feels right.

**Try this** (30 words)

Hold a piece of string or thread between two fingers as if it were hair. Practise the motion of trimming the end with your other hand. Repeat slowly five times.

### Listening to what clients want

**What this looks at work** (52 words)

Before you start, you ask the client how they want their hair, and listen until they finish. You repeat back what you heard so there are no surprises. Example: the client asks for a short trim, not a big change, and you confirm that before you cut. Never interrupt while they speak.

**Try this** (30 words)

Practise asking three questions a stylist might ask a new client, then repeat back what they would say. Say the whole exchange out loud, as if they sat down now.

### Managing appointment time

**What this looks at work** (53 words)

Appointments are booked times for each client. You keep to the booked length, and if one runs late, you tell the next client honestly. Good timing keeps the day running. Example: a colour treatment takes an hour, so you start it on time and set a timer. Clients wait less when timing works.

**Try this** (30 words)

Plan an imaginary day of four appointments with different lengths. Say what time each one starts and ends. Then imagine one runs late, and say how you would handle it.

### Knowing the products you use

**What this looks at work** (54 words)

Products are the creams, dyes, and shampoos you work with. You learn what each one does, how long to leave it on, and who should avoid it. Good advice builds trust. Example: a client asks which shampoo suits a dry scalp, and you explain two options clearly. Never recommend what you do not know.

**Try this** (36 words)

Pick one beauty or hair product at home. Say out loud what it does, who it suits, and one warning you would give a client before using it. Say why a client should trust your advice.

### Keeping your station welcoming

**What this looks at work** (53 words)

Your station is the chair, mirror, and counter where you work. Clients judge it before they sit down, so it stays tidy, clean, and friendly. You wipe the mirror, sweep hair away, and lay out clean tools. Example: a neat counter with fresh towels makes clients relax. A tidy station shows you care.

**Try this** (38 words)

Describe your ideal work station out loud, item by item: what is on the counter, what is on the floor, and what you would check before a client sits. Say what you would tidy just before they arrive.

## Working with computers

### Emails and attachments

**What this looks at work** (53 words)

Email is the main way offices share information. You write a clear subject, keep the message short, and check any file you attach actually attaches. You double-check the address before sending. Example: you attach a form, then confirm the paperclip icon appears before you press send. Attachments are files sent with the message.

**Try this** (34 words)

Write a short email on any scrap of paper to a colleague, asking them to send a form. Include a subject line, a clear ask, and a polite closing. Check it once before sending.

### Finding information online

**What this looks at work** (53 words)

Finding information means typing clear search words and checking where the answer came from. You look at the date, the source, and whether other sources agree. You never trust the first result blindly. Example: you search for office opening hours and check two websites before you phone. Wrong answers can cause real problems.

**Try this** (30 words)

Think of one question you want answered, like a bus timetable. Practise choosing the exact three or four words you would type into a search box. Say them out loud.

### Documents, slides and spreadsheets

**What this looks at work** (53 words)

These three tools cover most office work: documents for writing, slides for presenting, and spreadsheets for numbers. You learn to type, format, and save your work. Example: you write a short report, add three slides showing the main points, and list the costs in a spreadsheet. Save your work often as you go.

**Try this** (30 words)

Describe out loud how you would make a one-page document about your day. Say what you would type first, what you would format, and what you would name the file.

### Fixing small computer problems

**What this looks at work** (52 words)

Many computer problems have simple fixes: check the power, check the cables, and restart the machine. You try these calmly before asking for help. Example: the screen shows nothing, so you check the plug, check the cable, and restart before you call the IT person. Most small problems are solved right here.

**Try this** (35 words)

Say out loud the five calm steps you would take if a computer stopped working. Start with the simplest check and end with asking for help. Practise speaking slowly. Speak as if guiding a colleague.

### Protecting passwords and data

**What this looks at work** (52 words)

A password is a secret word that locks your accounts. You make it hard to guess, never share it, and never write it where others see. Some information stays private. Example: a customer’s phone number never gets shared with anyone outside the company, not even a friend. Careless sharing causes real harm.

**Try this** (32 words)

Think of one weak password and explain out loud why it is easy to guess. Then invent a stronger one: a short phrase of three or four unusual words you would remember.

### Confident typing

**What this looks at work** (52 words)

Confident typing means your fingers find letters without you looking down. You sit straight, rest your wrists, and aim for accuracy before speed. Faster typing saves time on every task. Example: you type a short note quickly, fix two mistakes, and finish before the phone rings again. Practise a little each day.

**Try this** (30 words)

Type or write out the same short sentence five times, as fast as you can while staying neat. Time yourself if you have a clock. Notice where you slow down.

## Caregiving

### Patience and respect

**What this looks at work** (55 words)

Care means treating every person with dignity, whatever their age or condition. You speak gently, move slowly, and give people time to answer. You never rush or talk over them. Example: an older person takes longer to dress, so you wait calmly and offer help only when asked. Kind words matter as much as tasks.

**Try this** (35 words)

Practise one respectful sentence you would use to offer help: "Take your time, I am here when you are ready." Say it out loud, slowly and kindly. Repeat it a little softer the second time.

### Hygiene and infection control

**What this looks at work** (51 words)

Infection control means stopping germs from spreading between people. You wash hands before and after care, use gloves when needed, and clean surfaces daily. You change gloves between tasks. Example: after helping one person, you wash your hands before you enter the next person’s room. People in care get sick faster.

**Try this** (35 words)

Practise the handwashing steps out loud: wet, soap, scrub palms, backs, between fingers, nails, then rinse and dry. Say each step slowly, as if teaching a new helper. This protects the people you care for.

### Helping with daily tasks safely

**What this looks at work** (54 words)

Daily tasks include washing, dressing, eating, and moving from a bed to a chair. You learn the right way to help so nobody falls. You bend your knees and keep the person close. Example: you help someone stand by counting to three together, then move slowly to the chair. You protect your back too.

**Try this** (33 words)

Stand up and mime helping someone rise from a chair. Say out loud each step: bend your knees, take their hand, count to three, then move slowly together. Say each step again slowly.

### Recording what you observe

**What this looks at work** (52 words)

You write down what you see: food eaten, sleep, pain, and any change in behaviour. Notes are factual and dated, with no opinions. Doctors rely on them. Example: you note that a person ate half their lunch and slept two hours, at the time it happened. Accurate notes help doctors decide well.

**Try this** (35 words)

Write three short factual notes about someone’s morning, as if for a care file. Keep each note under fifteen words, with a time. Avoid words like "good" or "bad". Add a date to each note.

### Following care instructions

**What this looks at work** (52 words)

Care instructions say exactly how to help someone: their medication times, their diet, and how they like to be treated. You follow them precisely and never guess. Example: medication is due at eight, so you give it at eight, and record that you did. If you are unsure, ask before you act.

**Try this** (31 words)

Think of three instructions a caregiver must follow exactly, like medicine times. Say each one out loud as a clear rule, in the words you would use on your first day.

### Staying calm in emergencies

**What this looks at work** (50 words)

In an emergency you breathe, think, and act in order: make the person safe, call for help, and follow your training. Panic makes everything worse. Example: a person falls, so you do not move them, you call your supervisor, and you stay with them. Staying calm helps you think clearly.

**Try this** (31 words)

Practise saying three calm sentences you would use in a crisis: calling for help, telling the person you are with them, and giving simple instructions. Say each one slowly and clearly.
